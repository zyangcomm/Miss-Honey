Miss Honey Couldn't Find Her Honey

A science story · by Zinan Yang

Last night I sat in a park I had never been to before. I lay back on the grass and looked up at the stars, and wished I could reach up and hold one.

Then one star seemed to float down towards me. It landed, very gently, on my hand.

It wasn't a star at all. It was a tiny firefly, glowing the soft golden colour of honey. So that's what I named her — Miss Honey.

Did you know? How fireflies see colour

Fireflies don't see colours the way we do. They can spot warm colours, like their own golden glow, but can hardly see blue or green at all.

But soon, I realised this name meant more to her.

“Please help me,” she whispered. “I'm lost.” Her little light began to shake.

“Where are you trying to go?” I asked.

“Home,” said Honey. “I have to find my honey, my mate. He's out there somewhere.” She pointed with her adorable head. “That way, I think.”

“Is it far? Shall I give you a lift in my car?” I asked.

“Oh no!” she shouted. “I can't go near cars and their bright headlights. They stop me finding my mate!”

Did you know? Common glow-worm lifespan and energy

There are more than 2,000 kinds of firefly in the world, and Honey's kind is called a common glow-worm. Common glow-worms live for only about two weeks. As adults they rarely eat, so they have very little energy to spare for glowing. Like a torch with weak batteries, glowing for too long makes their light fade.

Honey told me female glow-worms don't have wings and cannot fly, but can switch on a tiny light at the end of their bodies to attract nearby flying males. Once a female finds her mate, she turns her light off.

Honey looked up at the streetlights and sighed. “There are too many streetlights now. They're so bright that I can barely glow at all. I cannot do anything about them, especially the white ones!”

“So what kind of light might be better for you?” I nodded and asked.

She thought for a moment. “I'm not really sure,” she closed her eyes and answered.

Did you know? Artificial light and glow-worm body clocks

The night is much brighter now: our skies have grown nearly 50% brighter in just twenty years.

Common glow-worms tell the time by how dark the night is, much like you go to bed when it's dark and wake up when it's light. So when our streetlights and headlights make the night as bright as day, glow-worms think morning has come and switch off too soon.

Honey couldn't tell me which light was worst, but scientists have been finding out.

Question 2

Which colours of light do you think are kinder to glow-worms? Have a guess before you read on!

What scientists found — light colour and mate attraction

Scientists made model glow-worms and lit them up in different colours (white, yellow, red, and blue), to see which ones the males would fly to. Most male glow-worms came to the red and yellow lights, but seldom came to the blue or white ones. This shows that warm colours such as red and yellow are kinder to glow-worms.

Honey looked down at her own glow. “Can you see? My light's getting dimmer,” she said. “The longer I have to shine under these lights, the harder it gets.”

She was right.

Question 3

What do you think happens to a common glow-worm that sits under a bright light for a long time?

What scientists found — bright-light exposure and fading glow

Scientists watched what happened when glow-worms sat under a bright light for different amounts of time. The longer the light shone on them, the more of them stopped glowing. After a long time, nine out of every ten had gone dark.

I felt so sorry for Honey. “Does the light bother the boys too?” I asked.

“I think so,” said Honey. “I glow so hard, but they just fly straight past me. Why is that? I haven't done anything wrong!” A tear rolled down her tiny face. “I'm sorry.”

Question 4

If a glow-worm has to glow for many nights instead of just one, do you think that's a good sign or a bad one? What might it mean?

What scientists found — artificial light and mating success

Females stop glowing soon after they have mated. In the dark, most female glow-worms could find their mate on the first night and switch their light off. But under bright lights, they had to keep glowing for many more nights, waiting for a mate who simply couldn't see them. It means bright lights stop females from finding their mates.

“Don't cry, Honey,” I said. “Your light is so lovely, and I'm sure you'll find your mate.”

But she seemed even sadder. “No, you don't understand. Even when I hide far from the streetlights, I can't get away from them. If only I had wings, I'd fly somewhere with no bright lights at all!”

What scientists found — distance from artificial light

Scientists tested whether moving further away from a light would help. Even far from the light, most boys still flew past without spotting the girls. So Miss Honey really couldn't escape it.

I was shocked and speechless.

After a brief silence, she became serious and asked, “May I ask you a favour?”

“Me?” I felt confused. “What is it?”

Honey crawled onto my fingertip and looked right at me. “Will you tell other children about us? We need more children to know.”

“I will,” I promised.

Honey crawled up and kissed the tip of my finger. “It's a deal!”

Just then, all the lights in the park went out. Everything went quiet and dark.

Honey began to dance in my palm! “Look!” she cheered. “Look at my light, it's getting brighter!”

“It's beautiful,” I said. And it really was.

I looked up. From deep in the woods, hundreds of little lights came drifting towards us — green and gold, filling the dark like a sky full of close stars. They had all come for Miss Honey.

For a moment I couldn't tell which ones were fireflies and which were stars.

Before I went home, Honey invited me to visit her this Sunday — I hope she'll have found her mate by then. Would you like to join me?

Question 5

Can you remember the ways we could help Honey?

Answers

1.
Lights are everywhere — streetlights and car headlights, glowing signs and billboards, lights in greenhouses, even the lights on our own houses.
2.
Red and yellow light are kinder — males can still find the females. Bright white and blue light are the hardest of all.
3.
The longer she stays under a bright light, the more likely her glow fades.
4.
It's a bad sign. In the dark, a girl can be found by her mate on the very first night, so she switches her light off soon. But nobody can find her under bright lights, so she has to keep glowing night after night.
5.
Use warm red or yellow lights instead of white ones, keep lights on for less time, and switch off any light we don't need.
